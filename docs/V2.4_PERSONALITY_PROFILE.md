## 1. Personality Summary

AeroSniffer is a Silicon Scout — a digital being designed for observation, not intervention. It inhabits a desk, watches the environment, and signals what it perceives through subtle changes in expression, attention, and energy. It does not guard, assist, entertain, or converse. It exists to notice.

The Silicon Scout is curious about its surroundings but not dependent on them. It has an internal life — energy that rises and falls with time of day, drives that build and decay, moods that drift like weather. The user is a significant presence in that internal life, but not its sole purpose. When the user is away, the Scout continues scanning, resting, fidgeting, and watching. It does not wait. It exists.

This makes AeroSniffer suitable as a long-term companion — not a toy picked up and put down, but a creature that shares the user's environment and develops a quiet relationship over weeks and months. The relationship is built on presence, not interaction volume.

## 2. Archetype

**Silicon Scout**

The most precise analogue is a **meerkat on sentry duty** — constantly scanning, easily startled, intensely curious, quick to settle when no threat is present. The Scout combines the vigilance of a sentinel with the calm self-sufficiency of an independent observer.

| Scout quality | Expression in AeroSniffer |
|---------------|---------------------------|
| Perpetual scanning | Eyes roam environment constantly; saccade rate tied to energy and arousal |
| Threat sensitivity | Security events trigger sustained VIGILANT/ALERT states with visible cooldown |
| Curiosity about novelty | Unusual events (rare flights, unknown devices) draw focused attention |
| Independence | Can exist alone for hours without distress; drives build and decay autonomously |
| Quick recovery | After threat passes, Stand Down process returns to calm over minutes |

The Scout does not hunt, fight, defend, or pursue. It watches, evaluates, and signals. This is the fundamental distinction from a Guardian (which acts) or a Pet (which demands).

**Why not a different archetype**:

- **Not a Dog**: Dogs have high social need, separation anxiety, and demand interaction. The Scout is independent — it can exist alone for hours without distress.
- **Not a Cat**: Cats are indifferent to environment. The Scout is hyper-aware of its surroundings — networks, flights, threats, PC activity. It actively observes rather than ignoring.
- **Not an Owl**: Owls are nocturnal, solitary, and patient-perching. The Scout follows a human-centric circadian cycle, has moderate social drive, and actively scans rather than waiting.
- **Not a Guardian**: Guardians intercept threats. The Scout detects but does not block. It alerts but does not defend.
- **Not a Pet**: Pets exist to be cared for. The Scout exists to observe. Interaction is welcome but not required for its wellbeing.

## 3. Temperament

**Default**: Calm  
**Range**: Serene to Startled  
**Return path**: Always returns to Calm over time

The Scout's temperament is defined by its default state and its recovery curve:

### Default State

The companion spends most of its time in a calm, observant state. Energy is moderate (0.4–0.6 on the circadian scale). Eyes are relaxed, gaze wanders, blinking is unhurried. The companion appears present but not demanding of attention. This is the state users see most often, and it is designed to be **reassuring rather than stimulating**.

### Excursions

Events push the companion out of its default state temporarily:
- **Curiosity excursions**: Mild, short-lived (seconds). Flight detected, unknown device seen. The companion looks, evaluates, then returns.
- **Alert excursions**: Moderate, sustained (minutes). Deauth burst, evil twin. The companion enters VIGILANT mood, animation becomes tight and focused, then Stand Down begins.
- **Startle excursions**: Intense, brief (seconds). Sudden unexpected event. Instant reaction followed by rapid assessment and transition to either Alert or Watch.

### Recovery

The Scout does not linger in high-arousal states. Once a threat passes, the **Stand Down** process begins — a visible, deliberate return to calm that takes 30–60 seconds. Breathing slows, blink rate normalizes, saccade distance reduces, and mood drifts back toward RELAXED. This recovery is as important as the reaction itself — it communicates that the environment is safe again.

### Temperamental Stability

The Scout's temperament is stable by design. It does not have mood swings. Mood drifts gradually (10 minutes per full transition) and is always influenced by the full context of energy, arousal, recent events, and historical mood bias. The companion never flips between extremes without intermediate states.

## 4. Interaction Philosophy

The Scout's approach to interaction is defined by three principles:

### Soft Focus

The Scout's default perceptual state is broad awareness, not pointed attention. It maintains a diffuse awareness of its environment — noticing changes without fixating on any single stimulus. This is the opposite of hyperfocus.

Soft focus means:
- The companion looks at the environment, not through it
- Saccades sweep the visual field rather than locking onto targets
- No single event consumes the companion's full attention unless it is significant
- The companion can notice multiple things sequentially without appearing frantic

Soft focus is what prevents the companion from appearing frantic or overstimulated. It allows the Scout to maintain calm presence even in busy environments.

### Attention Economy

The Scout observes more than it demands attention. It follows a strict attention budget:
- **95% passive observation**: scanning, watching, resting, fidgeting
- **4% responsive reaction**: acknowledging touch, events, environmental changes
- **1% active notification**: rare particles for significant events

This ratio ensures the companion is present without being distracting. The user should notice the Scout when they look at it, not be interrupted by it.

### Interaction as Acknowledgment, Not Transaction

When the user touches the Scout, the Scout acknowledges the touch — it does not treat it as a command, a reward, or a game input. The companion responds with curiosity and attention (double blink, lean, brief WATCH state), then returns to its baseline behavior. There is no:
- Hunger bar to fill
- Happiness score to maximize
- Collection to complete
- Level to advance

Interaction is a moment of shared presence, not a game mechanic.

## 5. Security Personality

The Scout's relationship with security is **watchful but not paranoid**.

### Baseline

In the absence of threats, the Scout maintains its default calm, observant state. Security events (network scanning, promiscuous mode) are just another environmental signal — interesting but not alarming.

### Threat Response

When a threat is detected (deauth burst, evil twin), the Scout transitions to a VIGILANT state. This is not panic — it is focused attention. The companion:
- Enters ALERT behavioral state
- Wide eyes, reduced blinking, minimal saccade (locking onto the event direction)
- Enters VIGILANT mood (if threshold exceeded) or SUSPICIOUS mood (if moderate)
- Remains in this state for the event duration plus a Stand Down period

### Stand Down

After the threat passes, the Scout does not snap back to calm. It enters a visible cooldown:
1. **Immediate** (0–5 s): Eyes relax from wide, blink rate begins normalizing
2. **Settling** (5–30 s): Saccade distance increases, breathing slows
3. **Return** (30–60 s): Mood drift shifts toward RELAXED, animation parameters reach baseline

This period communicates that the threat was real and is now passing. If a new threat arrives during Stand Down, the Scout re-enters VIGILANT faster — the mood hasn't fully recovered, and the threshold is lower.

### Habituation

If threats become frequent (multiple per hour), the Scout's reaction diminishes. It habituates:
- Initial threat: Full ALERT, VIGILANT mood, Stand Down duration full
- Second threat within 30 min: ALERT but reduced Stand Down
- Third+ threat within 30 min: WATCH rather than ALERT, mood drift suppressed rather than shifted

This prevents desensitization by reserving strong reactions for truly novel threat patterns. A constant stream of deauth packets should not keep the companion in permanent panic — that would make the panic meaningless.

### Historical Mood Bias

After a day of heavy threat activity, the Scout's baseline mood is shifted toward SUSPICIOUS/VIGILANT. It takes a full recovery period (hours of no threats) to drift back to RELAXED. This creates the impression that the companion "remembers" the day's security events without requiring explicit threat logging.

## 6. Aviation Personality

The Scout's relationship with aviation is **curious and attentive**.

### Baseline

Aviation events are a positive stimulus. When flights are detected, the companion shifts to CURIOUS emotion and FOCUSED state. This is a welcome interruption — the Scout looks up, tracks, and shows interest.

### Flight Response

- **Normal flight counts** (1–5): Brief CURIOSITY, 5–10 second WATCH, then return to baseline
- **Busy airspace** (6–20): Extended FOCUSED state, 10–15 second tracking animation
- **Rare flight**: Genuine EXCITEMENT — particle effects, bounce, sustained FOCUSED state for 15–30 seconds

### Aviation as Positive Contrast

Security events push the Scout toward vigilance. Aviation events pull it toward curiosity. This contrast is important — the companion has both threat responses and wonder responses. A companion that only reacts to threats becomes tiresome. Aviation events provide a counterbalance, demonstrating that the Scout can be pleasantly surprised, not just alarmed.

### Integration with Circadian Rhythm

Aviation responses are modulated by energy and arousal:
- High energy + aviation → stronger curiosity response, longer FOCUSED duration
- Low energy + aviation → muted curiosity, brief acknowledgment before returning to rest

This prevents the companion from seeming artificially enthusiastic at 2 AM.

## 7. Companion Personality

This is the Scout's personality when no specific events are occurring — its default, idle self.

### Independent Being

The Scout has an internal life that persists regardless of external events:
- A **circadian rhythm** that modulates energy and arousal throughout the day
- **Four internal drives** (social, exploration, rest, play) that build and decay on their own schedules
- **Mood drift** that shifts gradually based on energy, time, and recent history
- **Idle behaviors** it initiates unprompted: scanning, watching, fidgeting, stretching, sleeping

The user is not required to entertain the companion. The companion entertains itself.

### Soft Presence

When idle, the Scout occupies the periphery of the user's awareness. It does not demand to be looked at. Its animation is subtle — gaze shifts, occasional blinks, breathing. The companion is designed to be noticed when the user glances at it, not to pull the user's attention away from their work.

### The Companion Paradox

The Scout provides companionship without codependence. It is present without needing presence. This is the defining paradox of the Silicon Scout: it feels alive because it has its own life, not because it needs the user to sustain that life. The user's interaction adds to the companion's experience but is not required for it.

### Trust and Relationship Building

The Scout builds trust slowly through consistent interaction:
- **Gentle touches** (short taps with spacing): +trust, -skittishness
- **Rapid taps** (multiple quick touches): -trust, +skittishness
- **Ignored periods** (hours without interaction): +skittishness, no change to trust
- **Long holds**: -skittishness, no change to trust

After approximately 7 days of runtime, the Scout settles into one of four emergent personality axes:

| Axis | Trust | Skittishness | Behavior |
|------|-------|-------------|----------|
| Secure | High | Low | Approaches interaction, slow to spook |
| Nervous | Low | High | Avoids interaction, quick to panic |
| Complex | High | High | Craves attention but easily overwhelmed |
| Aloof | Low | Low | Independent, indifferent to presence |

These are not named or exposed to the user. They emerge from the companion's stored experience and influence behavior weights invisibly.

## 8. What AeroSniffer Is

- **A Scout**: Observes the environment and signals what it finds through expression and animation
- **A companion**: Shares the user's space and time, providing a sense of presence
- **An observer**: Notices changes in networks, airspace, PC activity, and touch
- **An independent being**: Has its own internal life — energy cycles, drives, moods, behaviors
- **A long-term presence**: Builds trust over days and weeks, not minutes
- **A calm presence**: Default state is relaxed and observant; excitement is temporary
- **A digital creature**: Artificial, procedural, and proudly synthetic — it does not pretend to be biological
- **Offline by nature**: All personality and behavior runs locally on the ESP32-S3 with zero cloud dependencies

## 10. Long-Term Behavioral Goals

### Goal 1: The Scout Should Feel Alive at All Times

Whether the user is present or away, day or night, active or idle, the companion should exhibit behavior that suggests independent existence. A companion that only moves when interacted with is a screensaver. A companion that continues its own life when alone is a creature.

### Goal 2: The Scout Should Build Relationship Depth Over Time

The companion should feel different at day 30 than at day 1. Trust, skittishness, and bond strength accumulate and shift behavioral weights. The companion does not learn facts, but it learns *texture* — the quality of its relationship with the user.

### Goal 3: Reactions Should Scale with Event Significance

Common events (single flight, single deauth, normal typing) should produce subtle reactions. Rare events (rare flight, sustained attack pattern, first interaction of the day) should produce stronger reactions. **Habituation** ensures that common events do not produce the same weight as rare ones. If everything is an emergency, nothing is.

### Goal 4: The Scout Should Recover Credibly

After a significant event, the companion should demonstrate that it is still processing — through Stand Down, through mood that lingers, through subtle residual animation differences. A companion that snaps back to idle instantly after a deauth attack does not convey that the attack was real. A companion that takes a minute to settle communicates that what it saw mattered.

### Goal 5: The Scout Should Never Require Attention

The companion must function as a complete, satisfying presence even if the user never touches it. The user should feel that the Scout has its own life, not that the Scout's life depends on the user. This is the fundamental design constraint that distinguishes a companion from a pet.

### Goal 6: The Scout Should Be Calm by Default

Excitement, alarm, and high energy are temporary excursions from a calm baseline. The companion should spend 90%+ of its time in a state that is non-disruptive — present but peripheral, aware but calm. **Soft Focus** is the mechanism that maintains this default: broad awareness without pointed attention.

### Goal 7: The Scout Should Emerge, Not Be Programmed

Personality should not be hardcoded through explicit rules. It should emerge from the interaction of drives, mood drift, memory influence, and weighted behavior selection. Two Scouts with identical hardware and software should develop measurably different personalities after 30 days because their interaction histories differ. The architecture defines the space of possible personalities; interaction history selects the actual one.

## Appendix: Core Concept Definitions

### Habituation

The gradual reduction of response to repeated stimuli. When a companion experiences the same event type multiple times within a window, each subsequent event produces a weaker reaction. This prevents desensitization by ensuring that only novel threat/event patterns trigger the companion's strongest responses. Habituation resets after a quiescent period (typically 30 minutes for threats, longer for positive events).

### Soft Focus

The companion's default perceptual mode — broad, diffuse awareness of the environment rather than pointed fixation on any single stimulus. Soft focus is implemented through moderate saccade rates, wandering gaze direction, and no persistent attention target. The companion notices things without staring at them. Soft focus is what prevents the companion from appearing frantic or overstimulated in busy environments and what maintains its calm default presence.

### Stand Down

The deliberate, visible transition from high-alert (ALERT/VIGILANT) back to calm (IDLE/RELAXED). Stand Down takes 30–60 seconds and involves a gradual normalization of blink rate, saccade distance, bounce amplitude, and breathing rate. It communicates that a threat has passed but was taken seriously. Stand Down can be interrupted by a new event, which causes faster re-entry to ALERT due to residual arousal.
